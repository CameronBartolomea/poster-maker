# Concept Comparison

Three materially different visual directions were built and rendered
(not just described) before one was selected. Source files for all
three are in `source/three-cs-concepts/`.

## Concept A — Ascending Terrace (selected)
Three horizontal bands of increasing color intensity (light gray,
gray, near-black with red) stacked top to bottom, each wider in visual
weight than the last. No diagram, no boxes, no arrows -- the escalation
in color and weight IS the content: start quiet and respectful, only
grow bolder once it's welcome.

**Why it won:** the visual logic matches the actual teaching point
(don't lead with Contrast), it fills a full 8.5x11 page at premium
scale without feeling like a diagram, and it reads in under two
seconds. The one weakness found in review (accent-bar colors not
reading as a clear progression) was fixable without changing the
underlying structure.

## Concept B — Single Path
One continuous flowing line moves down the page and touches three
labeled circular nodes. Calmer and more organic than a flowchart.

**Why it lost:** the curved connector line read as closer to a process
diagram than intended, which the brief specifically asked to avoid
("no generic boxes and arrows unless there is a compelling visual
reason"). It was also harder to fill the full page height at the same
premium scale without the path feeling arbitrary.

## Concept C — Editorial Triptych
Three equal vertical panels, each with a massive ghost numeral behind
bold type. Pure typographic poster-making, no diagram at all.

**Why it lost:** the most purely "premium editorial" of the three, but
the three panels looked structurally identical to each other with no
built-in sense of progression -- exactly the "intuitive visual
progression" the brief asked for was the weakest part of this
direction, and fixing it would have meant converging toward Concept A
anyway (adding escalating color/weight across the panels).

## Evaluation criteria used
Professional quality, originality, visual impact, comprehension speed,
practical usefulness, artwork quality, typography, brand integration,
printed readability, natural language, ethical suitability -- per the
acceptance criteria Cameron set. All three initial drafts shared one
real weakness (significant unused page height at the drafted scale);
Concept A was rebuilt at true full-page scale as part of selecting it,
not just picked as-is.
