# Concept Comparison — Redesign Round 3

Per the acceptance criteria in the reference package: three materially
different fronts, each satisfying a different required emphasis, with
a candid comparative critique before finalizing.

Renders: `templates/three-cs-concepts-v3/`.

## Concept 1 — "Say This" (concrete spoken phrases)

Three icon-chip rows on tinted backgrounds, escalating gray to
charcoal to near-black. Each row leads with the exact quoted line at
dominant size (22px italic bold), a plain-language "why" underneath,
and a real pictogram (speech bubble, question mark, split circle) that
is literal, not a metaphor needing explanation. The stop condition is
a bordered callout attached directly to row 3, not deferred to the
back.

**Strengths:** warmest and most approachable of the three; the icons
read instantly without a caption; strong content density without
feeling cluttered.
**Weakness:** of the three, the one that leans most conventional —
closest in spirit to a well-executed version of what's already been
tried, even though the execution (real icons, real density, inline
stop condition) is well above the rejected version.

## Concept 2 — "Decision Path" (process / decision graphic)

A real flowchart, top to bottom: a start pill, step 01 (Compliment),
a dashed decision box ("Signed, exclusive agreement in place?"), a
branch to a bordered red STOP exit box on one side and a continuing
arrow on the other, step 02 (Clarify), a second decision box ("Do they
want to hear more?"), a second branch (a soft green "leave it open"
exit vs. continuing), and step 03 (Contrast).

**Strengths:** this is the most literal answer to the brief's own
suggestion ("a decision pathway with a represented-client exit") and
the most instructive piece of the three on the highest-stakes
question — a Realtor can see, structurally, that there are only two
places this can go, and exactly what happens at each one. The stop
condition isn't a footnote here; it's a first-class branch in the
diagram, same visual weight as every other step.
**Weakness:** more procedural in tone than the other two — it reads
like a well-made flowchart (which is the point), so it's slightly less
"an object someone wants on their desk" than Concept 3's editorial
confidence, even though it's fully legible and on-brand.

## Concept 3 — "Editorial" (ambitious visual direction)

A single oversized composition: the objection itself rendered large
and struck through ("I already have an agent."), replaced by a bold
reframe ("Good. That's where you start."), then three rows using
large numerals (46px) and a vertical color bar as the only graphic
device — closest in spirit to the reference set's own strongest move
(the struck-through rate number). No icons at all; pure typographic
hierarchy carries the whole page.

**Strengths:** the most confident, sophisticated-feeling composition
of the three, and the device (strike-and-reframe) is a genuine,
non-metaphorical technique borrowed in spirit from the references, not
copied from them. Likely the most "wantable to keep" as a piece of
design.
**Weakness:** the stop condition, while present and accurate, is
visually a secondary callout (a left-border note) rather than a
first-class structural element the way it is in Concept 2 — someone
skimming fast could miss it more easily than in the flowchart.

## Selected: Concept 2, "Decision Path"

The brief is explicit and repeated on this point: "a Realtor must know
what to SAY, when to proceed, and when to stop" is listed as the
specific bar for success, and a decision pathway with a
represented-client exit is named directly as a desired direction. Of
the three, this is the only one where the stop condition is
structurally unmissable rather than a callout competing for attention
with everything else on the page — and that's the single highest
compliance-stakes piece of content on this entire piece. It also tests
as the most "instantly useful within seconds" by the acceptance
criteria's own test, since the shape of the diagram IS the logic, not
a representation of it.

Concept 3 was the closer runner-up on pure visual sophistication and
remains available if Cameron prefers the more editorial, keep-worthy
object over the more procedural one — noting that explicitly rather
than silently dropping it.
